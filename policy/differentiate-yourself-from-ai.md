# Grading the Work Students Submit

{% include typography/section-accent.html %}

{% include typography/sketch-info.html %}

“Differentiate yourself from AI” asks students to meet a moving and poorly defined standard. A generic response may need revision, but its resemblance to AI doesn't explain what's wrong with it. The instructor needs to identify the missing evidence, unsupported inference, or interpretive choice that needs explaining.

This sketch proposes a revision to the approach described in [Start With the AI Answer](start-with-the-ai-answer.md), with wording to try in a future course.

## Making the expectation assessable

An assignment might ask students to:

- interpret a passage from the assigned reading;
- respond to a question raised in class;
- explain why a source supports a claim;
- consider another interpretation and give reasons for rejecting it;
- explain a revision made after feedback.

Choose the requirements that serve the assignment and state them before students begin. Those requirements give both the instructor and the student something to point to when discussing the work.

## Proposed syllabus language

{% capture policy_language %}
Each assignment explains what AI use is permitted and what work you must do yourself. Your submission must meet the stated requirements for evidence, interpretation, and explanation. If it falls short, I may ask you to revise or redo it. My feedback will identify what needs work and how the revision earns credit.

This applies whether or not you used AI. A polished, generic, or unfamiliar style is not itself grounds for a redo. You remain responsible for the sources you cite and the claims you make, and you must be able to explain your interpretive decisions.
{% endcapture %}

## What this can and cannot establish

These criteria let an instructor assess the submitted argument. They don't establish who produced it. A student could use AI to produce a response with quotations and specific claims, just as a student working independently could produce vague prose.

Some students have been taught to write generic academic prose; others are still learning a disciplinary voice or writing in another language. Feedback should give them something they can act on. “Explain how this passage supports your claim” identifies work to do. “This sounds like AI” leaves them guessing about the instructor's impression.

If the course needs to assess independent reading or drafting, it also needs occasions to observe that work. A final submission, however detailed, cannot supply the whole account of how it was made.

## Introducing it in a course

I'd begin with a low-stakes comparison of two sample responses. Ask students to locate the evidence and interpretive choices in each before applying the policy to a major assignment.

Then repeat the relevant expectation in individual assignments. “Discuss two passages from today's reading and explain how they support your claim” gives students a task they can understand. Decide in advance how revisions earn credit and explain that process alongside the standard.
